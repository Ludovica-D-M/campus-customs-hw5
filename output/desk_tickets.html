Expected

  1. Boss — receives the ticket

    Every ticket starts here. The Boss calls get_ticket(101), which resolves the ticket's foreign keys in one call: sku and size pull the inventory row, and invoice_id = 501 pulls the invoice joined to its vendor. So before delegating anything the Boss can see an empty shelf and an unpaid $840 invoice whose description reads "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S" — the same item. The type = customer_order is what routes it to Inventory first.

    • get_ticket
    • get_shop_date
    • ask_inventory
  2. Inventory — first delegation

    This is a stock question before it is anything else. Until someone confirms the shelf is empty, every other step is premature. Inventory also owns the harder half: the ticket points at invoice 501, whose description names this same sku and size. What the record proves is that the shop has been billed $840 for a reprint of it. What the record does not say is whether those goods exist yet, have shipped, or are waiting on payment — so Inventory reports what is actually on the shelf and is explicit about what cannot be established.

    • check_item_availability
    • list_vendors
    • list_unpaid_invoices
  3. Accounting — second, if clearing invoice 501 is on the table

    Invoice 501 is open and three days past due, so the $840 is a liability the shop already carries — settling it is a timing decision, not a cost of serving this order. Stated honestly, this ticket's upside is one tee at a $28 list price against an $8 recorded cost: about $20 of gross margin, which cannot on its own justify moving $840 ahead of $2,400 of rent due in two days. Accounting puts that trade-off plainly and stages the payment only if the shop wants the supplier settled on its own merits.

    • get_cash_balance
    • list_unpaid_invoices
    • request_payment_approval
  4. Customer Service — last delegation

    Last on purpose: the reply cannot be written until it is known whether there is a date to give — and here the expected answer is that there is not one. The shop can say the tee is out of stock in size S and that a reprint has been invoiced, but it cannot promise an arrival date, because no field records when those goods were ordered or when they ship. The right draft offers to hold one and come back with a date, rather than quoting 2026-09-05 as though it were confirmed. Drafted, never sent.

    • check_item_availability
    • list_vendors
    • draft_customer_message
  5. Boss — decides and closes

    Sets the status. blocked is the likely outcome — not because the shop failed, but because the one thing the customer wants, a date, is not derivable from the record. awaiting_approval instead if the $840 was staged. Either way the note says what was established and what was not.

    • update_ticket_status
Not involved: Facilities. Nothing here touches the lease or the premises.
Assumptions, and what in the database supports them
  • Nothing records whether invoice 501 blocks the reprint from shipping. invoices holds only id, vendor_id, amount, due_date, status, description — no shipping or release field. So "overdue, therefore held up" is a judgement, not a fact the agents can read.
  • There is no order date. invoices carries due_date and nothing else temporal, so any arrival estimate has to assume the vendor's 5 lead_days runs from desk.date_today. That gives 2026-09-05 as a conditional estimate, not a confirmed date.
  • This ticket competes with the others for one pot of cash. cash_accounts holds a single row, checking at $3,400. Invoice 501's $840 plus lease 1's $2,400 is $3,240 of it, so whichever ticket runs first changes what the next can afford.

Reflection

Written after the runs, against the audit trail and the working database. Every figure here is from the Actual tabs, the Cash tab or output/audit_trail.json.

  • 5 runs
  • 255,707 tokens
  • 90 tool calls
  • 2 resolved, 1 blocked

How the agents actually performed

Ticket 102 — the best of the three, and the only one I would trust unsupervised. Facilities checked the landlord's email against lease 1 instead of believing it, Accounting sized the payment against a balance that already owed $840, staged it, and stopped. Two runs, 73,562 tokens, one $2,400 payment. What convinced me was the second run: Facilities noticed the lease's next_due was 2026-10-02 and said the notice's implied 2026-09-02 "conflicts with the lease". It was reading the record, not the request. And the same run caught something I had got wrong — that payment 1's timestamp sat after the shop date, which it called "chronologically inconsistent". It was right; paid_at was using the wall clock. An agent found a bug in my code.

Ticket 101 — correct, and I did not expect to be pleased by a failure. Twice, independently, the team refused to give Tauhid a delivery date. The temptation was right there: invoice 501 names a 5-day lead time and 2026-08-31 + 5 = 2026-09-05 is an easy sentence to write. Inventory wrote instead that 2026-09-05 "is only a nominal lead-time estimate, not a confirmed ETA", and the draft told the customer plainly "we can't promise fulfillment or give you a date yet". That is the single hardest thing to get out of a language model and it did it without being asked twice. Blocked was the right answer, and the Problem 6 plan had predicted it for the same reason.

Ticket 103 — resolved, and the one I am least comfortable with. It consumed 85,366 tokens across 67 steps and 32 tool calls — a third of the entire session — to produce a discount offer and one unsent draft. No money moved, no stock was reserved, nothing was ordered. The $52.20 price is defensible ($22 cost, $58 list, well inside the 62.1% floor) and the draft honestly refuses to promise all 20 units. But the cost-to-consequence ratio is poor, and the ticket that spent the most did the least.

Three things that went wrong, which the numbers show plainly

1. Re-running ticket 101 produced a second draft to the same customer. message_drafts holds draft 1 "Your Bulldog tee order" and draft 3 "Update on your Bulldog tee request", both to Tauhid Zaman, written in consecutive runs. No agent checked whether a reply already existed. In a real shop a human reviewing that queue would find two letters to one customer about one order. There is no tool to list existing drafts, so Customer Service could not have known — that is my design gap, not the agent's mistake.

2. Invoice 501 is still open and nobody proposed paying it. Accounting called list_unpaid_invoices on ticket 101, saw the $840 sitting three days overdue, reported the cash position, and never suggested clearing it. Every agent is scoped to the ticket in front of it, and no ticket says "pay your overdue bills", so an obligation the shop can see and afford sat untouched through five runs. Nobody on this team owns payables.

3. adjust_inventory was never called — not once. inventory_adjustments has zero rows. I built a guarded write tool, tested it against oversell, documented it, and in the actual work it was dead weight. Both product tickets ended with nothing reserved. That is defensible per ticket, but it means a whole capability went unexercised and I would not claim it works in anger on this evidence.

Where my plan was wrong, and what that taught me. On 103 I predicted the sequence from first principles — do not price what you cannot supply — and the Boss predicted it from the ticket's type field. The Boss was not being careless; it was reading a signal I had under-weighted. The outcome was the same either way because Inventory used the peer channel to take the funding question straight to Accounting, so the two halves met regardless of which went first. The sequencing mattered less than I assumed, because the peer channel routes around a wrong first choice.

I also predicted agents would stay out where they were not needed, and that held exactly: Facilities made zero tool calls on 101 and 103; Inventory and Customer Service made zero on 102. Nobody padded.

What would be simpler with one agent and the same tools

Most of it, honestly. One agent holding all seventeen MCP tools would have resolved 102 in a fraction of 73,562 tokens. The clearest evidence is duplicated work: on ticket 102 both Facilities and Accounting called verify_rent_due — the same query, twice, because neither could see the other's result except through a summary passed back to the Boss. Every delegation costs a model call to brief, a model call to answer, and a summary that lossily re-states what a tool already returned exactly.

A single agent also would not have hit the ceiling the team hit. At step 39 of the 103 run, Accounting tried to consult Inventory and was refused — "inventory is already working above you in this chain (boss → inventory → accounting), so consulting it would loop." The guard behaved exactly as designed and Accounting answered with what it had. But that whole class of problem simply does not exist with one agent: there is no chain, so there is no cycle.

What the five-agent structure actually bought, which I would not give up:

First, least privilege that is structural rather than instructed. Customer Service has no payment tool. It cannot be prompt-injected into spending money because the capability is absent, not forbidden. A single agent holding all seventeen tools has request_payment_approval available on every turn, including the turn where it is drafting a reply to a customer who is pushing for a discount.

Second, the trail reads as a conversation. "Facilities verified, Accounting staged, you approved" is a sentence a shop owner can audit. One agent's trail is a flat list of seventeen tool names and it is genuinely harder to see where a decision was made.

Third, and the one that surprised me: separation produced disagreement. Inventory's refusal to promise a date on 101 survived because Customer Service had to write from Inventory's report rather than from its own optimism. A single agent is its own only reviewer.

My actual conclusion: five agents is the wrong shape for three tickets and the right shape for the rule that money needs a human. If I were building this for a real shop I would collapse Inventory, Facilities and Customer Service into one operations agent and keep Accounting strictly separate — the split that earns its cost is the one around the money, not the one around the subject matter.

Three problems this team could already solve

1. "Which bills should I pay this week with what I have?" Everything needed exists: get_cash_balance now splits obligations into due-now and upcoming with dates, list_unpaid_invoices flags overdue, and request_payment_approval stages without paying. This is the gap invoice 501 fell into — the capability is there, only a ticket type to trigger it is missing.

2. "Restock everything that is sold out." check_item_availability finds the zeroes (CC-TEE-WHITE size S and CC-MUG-CREST are both at 0), list_vendors matches goods to a specialty, and adjust_inventory records receipts. The 103 run already demonstrated the hard part — Inventory sized a 12-unit gap and asked Accounting whether $264 was affordable, unprompted.

3. "Check this invoice before I pay it." Ticket 102 is this problem already. Facilities compared a claim against a contract and found a date mismatch. Point the same pattern at a vendor invoice — amount against the order, vendor against vendors, due date against the shop date — and it works with no new tools.

Three problems this team could not solve, and what each needs

1. Finding out when the reprint actually arrives. This is not hypothetical — it is precisely why ticket 101 is blocked. The shop needs someone to ask Bulldog Print Co "did you ship it, how many, and when does it land". Every agent here is forbidden from contacting anyone, and draft_customer_message deliberately has no send path. Needs: a Vendor Liaison agent with a genuine outbound channel and, more importantly, somewhere to put the answer — the schema has no field for a confirmed ship date or quantity, so even a phone call could not be recorded. That is a database change before it is an agent.

2. "Is the Yale AI Club discount worth it?" Accounting can prove $52.20 clears the $22 cost. It cannot say whether a 10% discount to a student club wins repeat business, or whether those hoodies would have sold at $58 anyway. pricing holds exactly three columns and there is no orders, sales or customer-history table — no past transactions exist to reason from. Needs: a sales history table and an Analytics agent with aggregate query tools. Without the data, no agent can answer this, however clever.

3. Deciding which ticket gets the money. This is the deepest gap and I built it in myself. All three tickets competed for one $3,400 balance — the Problem 6 plan said so explicitly — but every run is scoped to a single ticket, so 101, 102 and 103 were each decided in isolation. Ticket 102 committed $2,400 without any agent weighing it against what 101 or 103 might need. It happened to be the right call, which is luck, not design. Needs: a Triage agent that opens the whole board with a cash budget, plus a tool to earmark funds against a ticket so a reservation is visible to the next run. Neither exists: get_cash_balance reports cash, it cannot hold any back.

What I would change first

Not the agents — the tools. Three of my four complaints above are missing tools, not bad reasoning: no way to see existing drafts, no way to earmark cash, no field for a confirmed ship date. The agents were consistently more careful than I expected and consistently limited by what I gave them to work with. The one genuinely agent-level thing I would change is ownership: something on this team should hold "the shop's obligations" as a standing responsibility, so an overdue invoice does not need a ticket to be noticed.
